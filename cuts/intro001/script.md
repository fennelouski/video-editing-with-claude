## 5. What yeast actually is — 3:27

> So what is yeast, in and of itself? Yeast in and of itself is a small little bug. It's a
> fungi. It's a single-cell organism. It's eukaryotic.
>
> It reproduces itself by budding, where a mother cell will start to grow a little bud off of it
> for a daughter cell. And the daughter cell, once it gets big enough — mitosis happens, and it
> splits, and then it continues to grow, and those split, and those split. And that is how
> *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* splits and grows and reproduces.
>
> All over — we use it everywhere, from our home bakeries to the brewers down the road to the
> vineyards in California and France. Everywhere. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. Same guy, again
> and again and again.

**Graphic — the budding cycle.** Mother cell, bud, daughter, split; then those two split.
This is the molecular kit's first real job. Four to six variants of each cell so no two are
identical, stepped on 2s, and the split should land on a beat in her delivery rather than on a
timer.

**B-roll.** A microscope shot of live budding yeast is the obvious want and the hardest to get.
Worth checking whether a university lab nearby will let you point a camera down an eyepiece for
an afternoon — it would carry this entire section and could be reused forever.

## 6. What it needs — 4:24

> So it will bud and reproduce in an environment that it thrives in. It likes it to be
> nutritionally dense for it — that's where it likes having starches and sugars to break down,
> and give the energy that it needs to continue to grow.
>
> It likes its temperature between 54 degrees Fahrenheit and just over 100 degrees Fahrenheit.
>
> Underneath 54 degrees, it goes back into a little shell of itself and hibernates. It just
> tucks away and takes a nap until it warms back up and is a good environment for it to grow and
> thrive. And that's how it survives not being able to move from environment to environment.
>
> Over 100 degrees Fahrenheit it can still grow, until it gets to about 140 degrees Fahrenheit —
> and that's when baking happens, and it kills it off in the oven. You might get oven spring
> from the yeast still growing, and other things happening, but then it stops. And you're not
> eating fresh yeast when you eat bread, because you've killed it off.

**⚠︎** 54 °F / 100 °F / 140 °F. Three numbers, one graphic, all three need her sign-off.

**Graphic — the temperature scale.** One vertical strip: 54 °F at the bottom, "just over
100" as the happy band, 140 °F as the kill line. The thermometer cutout is already in the §1.1
kit list. Numbers on screen mean **D3 has to clear first**.

**B-roll.** Dough going into a hot oven, and the rise happening in the first minutes — oven
spring is a thing she names that almost nobody has actually *seen*. A glass-door oven and a
timelapse would do it.

## 7. What it does — the chemistry — 5:24

> So you have your starch, and it breaks down with alpha amylase and beta amylase — and we'll
> get into that in another video. And then that breaks down to sucrose. And sucrose exists
> around the yeast, and the yeast itself provides invertase. And invertase is an enzyme — if it
> ends in "-ase", it's an enzyme. And so the invertase goes in to sucrose and breaks it down
> into glucose and fructose.
>
> You get zymase, and it breaks it down into your CO₂ and your alcohol. That's your carbon
> dioxide and your alcohol.
>
> And then when you bake the bread, the alcohol and the CO₂ kind of burn off and leave the
> bread, so they're not left behind in the bread after you've cooked it. And you also have your
> CO₂ — that's where you get your bubbly wines, like your champagne.

**⚠︎ This section is the weakest thing in the footage, and she says so on tape.** Immediately
before the first usable take she says *"I think this is where I'm missing — my notes are not
complete enough"*, and she qualifies the invertase step with *"I believe"*. Three separate
things are wrong or missing here and none of them are the pipeline's to fix:

1. **Starch → sucrose is not a step.** Amylases cut starch toward maltose; the sucrose in a
   dough is mostly what was already in the flour or what you added. As cut, it says the amylases
   produce the sucrose that invertase then splits.
2. **The payoff line is missing.** She reaches *"and that is why we use yeast in our foods —
   because those are the byproducts…"* and trails off without finishing it. It is cut, so the
   section ends on champagne instead of on the point.
3. **The alcohol beat is garbled.** *"But the alcohol is left behind into… your alcohol"* — the
   contrast she wants (it bakes off in bread, it stays in beer and wine) never lands. Cut.

The rest of the video works without this section. It should be re-shot against confirmed notes
rather than patched in the edit.

**Picture.** Her to camera, reading from notes, and it shows — this is the section she
flags on tape.

**Do not build these graphics yet.** The chain as cut has a factual problem (D4) and a hole where
the payoff line should be (C2), and a diagram would harden both. Once it is re-recorded, this

**B-roll, whenever it is reshot.** Bubbles under a loaf's skin. And champagne — she names it, and

## 8. What slows it down — 6:20

> In your dough, you have fats, salts, spices. It kind of gets in the way between the yeast and
> the sugar and the starches that it wants to eat. And so it slows it down. It takes longer.
> It's harder for the yeast to reach its food source. It can get there — it just takes more
> time.
>
> So when you're cooking with added fat, salt, spices to a dough, you just need to factor in
> that it's going to take more time for it to proof, for it to get to what it needs. Because it
> needs starches, sugars, it needs water. That's all it needs.
>
> A pre-ferment is flour, water, yeast. That's all that's needed.
>
> I know some yeast says it needs sugar to activate — active dry yeast, they recommend that.
> Really, if you give it time, you don't need the sugar in it. You don't have to add the sugar
> to your dough, unless you want a sweeter dough.
>
> So it's just: how much time do you want? If you need to bake dough and you want it to rise a
> little bit faster, you can add sugar to it. It's just going to be a sweeter bread — but it
> will rise a little faster, get going a little faster, shorten your proofing time.

**B-roll.** Fat, salt and spice going into a bowl, shot close. And the pre-ferment being mixed —

## 9. So why are there so many kinds? — 7:31

> *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* — I said it's all the same thing. But why? Why do we have so many
> different forms, when it's all the same guy?
>
> Well, he likes to go dormant. He might die. He needs access to food sources. It comes down to
> how frequently does the person using it need to have access to the yeast.
>
> A baker is using yeast day in, day out. They can have fresh yeast on hand, because they might
> not want to wait for a dry yeast to come building back up into active yeast that is moving and
> flowing. Having fresh yeast will shorten their cook time, their baking time, their proof time,
> and it makes it easier.
>
> But if you have a baker in Kansas that lives on a farm that takes an hour to get to their
> local grocery store — having fresh yeast is not practical, because they need something that
> can stay on hand, that lasts longer. That's where you get your active dry and your instant
> yeast. It's a casual baker, a person that doesn't want something that is gonna go and die
> within a couple weeks, a week. They need something that lasts longer, because of the effort it
> takes to get to the grocery store.
>
> Also, those grocery stores probably don't sell a lot of yeast, because they might not have as
> frequent customers. And so having fresh yeast on hand, and having it die on hand again and
> again, might not be financially viable. So they have the dry yeast on hand, so that way when
> the customer comes in it's available, it's not dead. It's what those people need.
>
> But if you have yeast on hand, and you have someone that's wanting to learn how to brew,
> they're searching — how do I find yeast? And they get brewer's yeast. "Yes, I'm brewing, let
> me buy brewer's yeast." It's marketing sometimes. Sometimes it's convenience. Sometimes it's
> who the customer is. But if you know what you're looking for, you can buy what you can find
> and work with it.

**Picture.** Her to camera for eight minutes of runtime by this point. This section needs
the most help and has the least obvious imagery.

**Graphic — the two bakers.** A working bakery on one side, a farmhouse an hour from a shop on
the other, and the same organism sold in two different forms between them. Drawn as a pair of
cards rather than photographed; it is an argument, not a place.

**B-roll.** A supermarket baking aisle — the actual shelf, with the actual small yeast section —
is a cheap shot that lands her point about shelf economics better than any drawing.

## 10. Instant, active dry, and glutathione — 9:31

> If you're really wanting to deep dive into making the best, most perfect bread, the most
> premium bread — you might care what type of yeast, because there is a little bit of a
> difference with instant and active dry.
>
> And so the yeast might become damaged from sitting and being dried out. And when it comes up
> and back to life, it can release something called glutathione. And what that can do is, it can
> get into the gluten matrix and attack it a little bit.
>
> So you have your instant dry yeast — what you might find is, it's available, it's findable,

>
> And so day to day, does it really matter? Probably not, with the bread that you want to make.

> stretchy, it's not as elastic as you want the bite to be — try seeing if you can find fresh
